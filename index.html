Chapter Summary

Understand → Connect → Remember

India’s economic journey can be remembered as a shift from a historically important economy to a colonial economy marked by exploitation and near-stagnation, followed by planned development after independence, faster growth during the 1980s, major LPG reforms after the 1991 crisis, strong expansion before the 2008 global financial crisis, and another major structural shock from COVID-19. Angus Maddison’s estimates place India and China together at 50.5% of global GDP in 1000 AD, while India alone accounted for about 23% in 1600 AD; by 1947, India’s global income share had fallen to around 3%. British rule was associated with deindustrialisation, agricultural commercialisation, land-revenue extraction, wealth drain and colonial trade policies. After independence, growth remained around 3.5% during 1951–1980, a pattern Professor Raj Krishna described in 1978 as the “Hindu Rate of Growth”. Growth improved in the 1980s and accelerated after the 1991 Liberalisation–Privatisation–Globalisation reforms. Following the 2008 global financial crisis, fiscal and monetary stimulus supported recovery. The COVID-19 shock then severely affected contact-intensive services, manufacturing, construction and employment, while agriculture remained relatively resilient. The longer-term structural story is the declining relative contribution of the primary sector and the growing dominance of services, which contributed about 60.9% of the Indian economy in 2020–21.
